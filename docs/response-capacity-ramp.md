# Response-capacity ramp library: method and limits

Companion to `lovs/forecast/capacityramp.py`. Written against the frozen
extract, with every figure below generated by that module: the tables are
emitted by `python3 -m lovs.forecast.capacityramp` and
`tests/test_capacity_ramp.py` fails if a number in this file is not the number
the module produces.

## Why this exists

Every outbreak response ramps its pillars from nothing to functional. Contact
tracing, alert triage, laboratory throughput, isolation beds: each starts at no
capacity and arrives, weeks later, at something that works. How many weeks is
the first question a health ministry or a funder asks, and there is no
published answer, because the ramp is measured inside each response and
reported as a status rather than as a duration.

The operational series makes it measurable for one outbreak. The point of doing
it as a LIBRARY rather than as a report is that one outbreak has no error bar.
The row shape carries the outbreak label, the declaration date, both clocks and
the censoring state, so the next outbreak appends its rows and the pair becomes
a distribution instead of an anecdote.

## Substrate

`data/operational-series-2026-09-01.json`: a frozen tidy extract of 101

the operational forecaster uses. Declaration is taken as 2026-05-15. As-of is a
parameter; omitted, it is the extract's own stamped extraction date, so
staleness is a property of the data rather than of when the module runs.

`lag` is days from declaration to the pillar's first observation. `stale` is
days from the pillar's last observation to the as-of date. A pillar is called
`stopped` rather than `live` when its trailing silence is at least twice the
longest silence it has previously recovered from, with a six-day floor, which
is a rule derived from each pillar's own cadence rather than a date typed in
here.

## The ladders, and what each proxy cannot see

<!-- generated: rationale-block -->
**contact tracing** (`contact_followup_percent`, percent, >= 50, >= 70, >= 80, >= 85)

- Ladder: 80 percent is the follow-up level filovirus response plans conventionally write down as the target. This module does not verify that convention against a source and does not treat it as a finding. 50 and 70 bracket it from below so the ladder shows the SHAPE of the approach rather than one pass/fail, and 85 sits above it so a response that overshoots the convention is distinguishable from one that merely meets it.
- Proxy limit: The denominator is contacts on the register. A register that misses contacts raises the rate; this metric cannot see that.

**alert triage** (`alert_investigation_rate_percent`, percent, >= 50, >= 70, >= 80, >= 85)

- Ladder: Deliberately the same ladder as contact tracing. Two percent pillars on one ladder is the only way the library can say which pillar stood up first without the answer being an artifact of two threshold choices.
- Proxy limit: Alerts investigated over alerts reported. Both move; a fall in alerts reported raises the rate with no change in investigative capacity.

**testing breadth (ascertainment proxy)** (`lab_positivity_percent`, percent, <= 30, <= 25, <= 20, <= 15)

- Ladder: The ladder descends because improvement is downward: high positivity means testing is confined to the most obviously ill. The rungs are the quartiles of the conventional 10-to-30 band rather than a published target, because no positivity target exists for filovirus outbreak testing that this module could cite.
- Proxy limit: Positivity falls when the testing net widens and when incidence falls. A milestone here is consistent with both, which is why it is read alongside samples_analyzed: a fall in positivity against a rising denominator is the only version of it that is about ascertainment.

**laboratory throughput** (`samples_analyzed`, samples per day, >= 100, >= 200, >= 300, >= 400, >= 500)

- Ladder: Round hundred-sample capacity steps. There is no defensible per-outbreak target to anchor to, so the rungs are deliberately arbitrary and evenly spaced: the transferable quantity is the SPACING between them (how long a lab takes to add a hundred samples a day), not the rungs themselves.
- Proxy limit: Throughput achieved, not throughput available. It is bounded above by demand: a lab that could run 600 and receives 400 reports 400.

**isolation capacity** (`hospital_isolation_total`, patients in isolation, >= 300, >= 500, >= 750)

- Ladder: Three rungs rather than five because the series spans 258 to 896 and a finer ladder would report noise as milestones. 300/500/750 are round steps inside the observed range.
- Proxy limit: OCCUPANCY, NOT CAPACITY. This counts patients in isolation, so it is a lower bound on beds that existed and it rises with demand as readily as with supply. A rising ramp here is not unambiguously good news.

## Method

**A hold, not a touch.** A milestone is `hold_n` consecutive observations at or
beyond the threshold, 5 by default. Contact follow-up printed
71.8 on 2026-06-10 and 28.4 on
2026-06-11; a library built on single-day touches would have dated the 70
percent milestone to day 26 instead of day 37, wrong by 11 days. 5 consecutive
observations is the smallest rule that survives a single-day collapse in either
direction while still fitting inside a fifteen-week record for a pillar that
reports on four days in five. Both numbers stay on the row: `first reach` is
when it was first seen met, `hold start` is when it began the run that stuck.

**Two clocks, never merged.** Days since declaration and days since the
pillar's first observation are different measurements. Here they differ by
between 6 and 21 days depending on the pillar, because pillars enter the SitRep
at different times. The declaration clock is what a ministry means by "how long
did it take"; the pillar clock is what an implementer means by "how long did it
take once we started reporting". Quoting one as the other charges a reporting
lag to the response, or hides it entirely. Every row carries both.

**Nothing is interpolated.** Where a reporting gap straddles a crossing, the
milestone is dated to the first day it was SEEN met and the row carries the
width of the window it could actually have happened in
(`first_reach_window_days`) with `first_reach_bound` set to `interval`. Where
the pillar's first observation already meets the threshold, the crossing
happened before anyone was reporting, the declaration-clock figure is an upper
bound and nothing else, and `first_reach_bound` is `left_censored`.

**Ramp and plateau.** The ramp phase runs from a pillar's first observation to
the hold start of the most demanding threshold it ever held; the plateau is
everything after. The ramp slope is least squares over the observed points
rather than endpoint to endpoint, because an endpoint slope is hostage to two
observations and this series is known to produce single-day artifacts. The
median per-elapsed-day step is reported beside it, reusing the forecaster's gap
normalisation, because the two disagree in a way that is itself informative.

`plateau drift` is the fitted plateau slope multiplied by the plateau's own

deviation, the plateau is still trending, and calling it a plateau is a
misreading.

## Findings

**The best-looking pillar is the one with no measured milestone.** Alert triage
is at or above every rung on this ladder the first time it is reported, so all
four of its rows are left censored: the ramp happened during the 20 days before
the indicator entered the SitRep, and the library reports upper bounds and
refuses to fit a slope. A table that dropped the censoring flag would show
alert triage standing up faster than every other pillar. It shows nothing of
the kind. It shows that nobody was reporting it while it stood up.

**The most valuable single number is the one the record cannot pin.** Contact
tracing has the best coverage of any pillar and the earliest first observation,
and its 50 percent crossing, plausibly the most transferable figure in the
whole exercise, falls inside a 14-day reporting gap. The library reports the
date it was first seen met and the width of the window, and does not choose a
date inside it. That is the honest answer, and it is less useful than the
answer a less careful method would have produced.

**Held is not stable, and the gap between them is wide.** Contact tracing held
80 percent from day 41 and then missed 80 percent on 22 of its next 60
observations. Isolation held 750 and missed it on 28 of 50. 6 of the 16 rungs
that held carry the same warning. A library that published only
days-to-threshold would export a picture of a response that stood up and stayed
up, and that is not what this record shows: it shows first attainments, several
of which the response then spent months oscillating around.

**The only degrading plateau belongs to the only pillar that stopped
reporting.** Alert investigation drifts -10.5 percentage points across its
plateau, more than its own standard deviation, and then the series ends on
2026-08-05 with 27 days of silence to the as-of date. Those two facts are
stated next to each other because they are both true. The library does not
claim one caused the other, and cannot: an indicator that degrades and an
indicator that disappears look identical after the last data day.

**The laboratory ramped in batches, not on a slope.** Least squares puts
laboratory throughput at +4.968 samples per day per day across its ramp, while
the median per-elapsed-day step over the same phase is
-5: on most days the lab processed fewer samples
than the day before, and the whole increase came from a minority of large
jumps. Any single-number ramp rate for a count metric hides that shape, which
is why both are reported.

**The ascertainment reading survives its own caveat, narrowly.** Positivity
moved 30 to 14.4 across its record while laboratory throughput moved 120 to 569
across the same window, so the fall in positivity is not a collapse in testing.
It remains consistent with falling incidence as well as with widening
ascertainment, and this library cannot separate those two.

## Appending the next outbreak

`library()` takes rows, an outbreak label, a declaration date and a pillar spec
set. A second outbreak is a second call; `compare()` lines the two up by
(pillar, threshold) and marks a pair `comparable` only when both rungs are
scale free and neither figure is left censored. Two count-metric milestones
from outbreaks of different sizes are not a comparison, and two upper bounds
are not a difference.

The transferable quantities, in the order this record supports them:

1. Rate-metric rungs (contact tracing, alert triage, testing breadth), which are
   scale free and directly comparable where they are not censored.
2. The SPACING between count-metric rungs, which is a ramp rate rather than a
   level.
3. The count-metric levels, which are not transferable at all and are marked
   `scale_free=False` on the row.

## Reproducing

    python3 -m lovs.forecast.capacityramp            # the blocks in this file
    python3 -m lovs.forecast.capacityramp --write    # refresh them in place
    python3 -m lovs.forecast.capacityramp --json     # pillar and curve rows
    python3 -m pytest tests/test_capacity_ramp.py -q

`--write` replaces only the fenced blocks and leaves the prose alone. The
figures the prose states outside a table are checked one by one by
`test_inline_figures_in_the_prose_are_the_module_figures`, so neither half of
this document can drift from the module without the suite failing.
